What this is

  • A rehearsal for January 20. Every skill from the last five weeks, used once, in order.
  • Graded on whether your team shipped, together, on time. Not on how clever the idea is.
  • Do it in one sitting of three hours, any time from Dec 31 to Jan 5, with everyone present, in person or on a call.
Why we do thisA tiny product that works and is live beats a big one that does not. The point is to learn where your team gets stuck, while it is safe to get stuck.

Say

  • Say plainly that it is not a competition between teams. Each team is competing with its own clock.
  • The best outcome is a team that discovers a real problem, like a deploy that fails with two committers, and writes it down.

Watch for

  • Do not let a team treat the three hours as a suggestion and spread it across a week. The time pressure is the lesson.

01 — The brief

Pick one of three, or propose your own

You may propose your own if the whole team agrees it follows the rules on the next slide. Decide in 15 minutes. Do not debate for an hour.

idea A

A. Turn it into a plan

Paste a messy to-do or homework list, get a clean evening schedule.

idea B

B. Explain it back

Paste your notes, get five quiz questions to test yourself.

idea C

C. Find it fast

A short list of places or things near school, filterable in two taps.

Say

  • The three ideas are deliberately small. They each have one user, one input and one output, so the work is in shipping and not in inventing.
  • A team that proposes its own idea should be asked the Week 5 question: what is the one flow, and what are the three screens?

Watch for

  • The AI feature is optional. If a team wants a model in the product, they can use the Week 4 pattern with the offline fallback. A working product without AI beats a broken one with it.
01 — The brief

Rules the product must follow

  • One user, one flow, three screens. Start, do the thing, see the result
  • Works on a phone, opens in a private window, no login
  • A real live URL, not a localhost link and not a screenshot
  • An AI feature is welcome and not required
  • No real personal data anywhere. Use made-up names and examples when you test
  • No payments, no accounts, no scraping anyone's website

Say

  • The no real personal data rule protects them and their friends. Tell them to make up names for every test.
  • No login keeps the scope small and keeps a whole class of security mistakes out of a first project.

02 — The clock

Spend the three hours like this

0-15 min
Agree the ideaOne sentence, one user, one flow
15-30 min
Repo and skeleton liveIssues on the board. Hello-world on a real URL
30-120 min
Build the hero flowAt least two PRs, each reviewed by someone else
120-150 min
Polish and test like a judgeEmpty states, errors, phone, private window
150-170 min
Freeze. Record the demoNo new features. 60-second video
170-180 min
Retro and hand inFive questions, then post the links

Say

  • The two orange bars are where teams usually lose time: choosing an idea for too long, and leaving deployment until the end. A live skeleton in the first half hour means deployment problems show up early, when they are cheap to fix.
  • Notice the freeze. After minute 150 nobody adds a feature, no matter how good the idea is.

Watch for

  • Teams will want to spend the first hour choosing. Fifteen minutes. If they cannot agree, flip a coin and move on.
02 — The clock

Checkpoints, and what to cut

MinuteThis must be trueIf it is not
30A skeleton page is live on a real URLStop building. Fix deployment first. Nothing else matters until this works
75The hero flow works on your own laptop, with fake dataCut the AI part. Hard-code the answer and finish the screens
120The hero flow works on the live URLCut every screen except the one the demo needs
150Nothing is half-finished on mainRevert or delete the unfinished part. Freeze

Say

  • Read the right-hand column aloud to yourself before you start. Cutting scope is a skill, and the middle of a sprint is the worst time to decide when to do it.
  • On a real hackathon day these same checkpoints scale up. The rule does not change: if you are behind, cut.

Watch for

  • Cutting the AI part feels like failure. It is not. A judge cares that the product works.

When it goes wrong

What happenedDo this
A merge conflictDo not panic. Open the file, read both sides, decide together (Week 3)
The deploy failsRead the last lines of the build log. Missing package? Wrong case? (Week 5)

The README, in a dozen lines

One sentence: who it is for, what it does.

## Who did what

## What AI did

## What we cut
- ...
  • The live URL is on line three, where a judge looks
  • Say who did what. Honestly. Everyone has a line
  • What we cut shows judgement, which is a good thing
RememberA judge who opens your repo reads the README and nothing else.

Say

  • The README is the second thing a judge opens after the demo. It should work with the video off.
  • This template will also be in the templates folder.
03 — Hand it in

A 60-second demo video

  1. 0-10 s The problem, in one sentence, for one person
  2. 10-45 s Use the product, live, on a phone. One flow
  3. 45-55 s What you would build next
  4. 55-60 s Say the URL
  • Record the screen with the phone's screen recorder or your laptop's
  • No music, no effects, voice only
  • Do one take, then maybe a second. Not ten
  • It is also your backup if the live demo breaks

Say

  • The video has two jobs: it is the hand-in, and it is the backup that saves you when the Wi-Fi dies on demo day. Teams should practise making one now.
  • Voice only is the easiest to record and the easiest to listen to.

Watch for

  • Do not accept a video that is two minutes of explanation and ten seconds of product. The product has to be on the screen.
03 — Hand it in

The five-question retro

Blameless. Name problems, not people. This is the most valuable part of the sprint.
