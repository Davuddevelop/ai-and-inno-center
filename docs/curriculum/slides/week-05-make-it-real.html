01 — The interface

Judges meet the interface first

question 1

What is this?

Within five seconds.

question 2

What do I do?

One obvious action.

question 3

Does it work?

A result, not a spinner.

The ten-second testIf a first-time user cannot answer all three in ten seconds, a judge will not either. They have a room of other projects to see.

Say

  • Judges see ten or twenty projects in a day. The interface is where they decide in ten seconds whether to lean in.
  • These three questions are the test you apply to your own screen.

Do

  • Put a screenshot of a real, plain page on the projector and give the room ten seconds. Ask them the three questions.

One flow, three screens

StartWhat is this, and the one button

Do the thingThe input, then the work
wait
See the resultThe answer, clear and shareable
  • Write the user story: 'A student wants to ___ so that ___.'
  • Design the one path through it. Everything else is out of scope.
  • Plan the four states of each screen: empty, loading, error, done.
An example worth copying'A student wants to find a free study room right now, so that they stop texting five friends.' One sentence, one flow, three screens.

Say

  • The hero flow is the thing your demo shows. Everything on the screen should serve it.
  • The four states are where hackathon interfaces fall apart. A screen that is great when full and blank when empty looks broken in a demo.

Do

  • Ask each team to say their hero flow in one sentence. If it has the word 'and' twice, cut it.

Watch for

  • Teams will add a login screen and a settings page. Neither is on the hero flow. Cut them.
01 — The interface

A wireframe in fifteen minutes

  1. Paper or Figma. No colour, no fonts. Boxes and words only
  2. Biggest thing on the screen = most important thing
  3. One primary button per screen
  4. Show a teammate for 10 seconds. Ask them what they would tap
Why so roughFix the layout before you fix the colours. A wireframe is cheap to change. A built page is not.

Say

  • Do this on paper if you like. The tool does not matter. What matters is that you decide the layout before you ask an AI to build it, so you are steering.
  • Showing it to someone for ten seconds is the cheapest usability test there is.

Do

  • Everyone sketches the three screens of their own idea, on paper, in five minutes. Swap and ask the neighbour what they would tap.
01 — The interface

Design basics that carry a demo

RuleIn practice
ReadableBody text at least 16px. Strong contrast (a 4.5:1 ratio is the standard for normal text)
TappableButtons about 44px tall so a thumb can hit them
ConsistentOne spacing scale. Two fonts at most. One accent colour
RealReal words and real data in the demo, not 'Lorem ipsum'
ResponsiveIt must work on a phone. Test it on one
The two-second phone checkOpen your page on your phone. If you have to zoom, or scroll sideways, fix that before anything else.

Say

  • None of these needs talent. They are rules you can check. A plain page that follows them beats a fancy one that breaks them.
  • The contrast number is a real accessibility standard: 4.5 to 1 for normal text. A free contrast checker in the browser will tell you.

Watch for

  • Judges are often reading your screen from a projector or a laptop across a table. Small grey text disappears.
01 — The interface

AI-made interfaces and how not to look generic

AI interface generators are fast and they all drift to the same look. Use them for the structure, then make it yours.

what judges have seen a hundred times

The tells

Purple-to-blue gradients. Glowing blobs. Emoji as icons. A centred hero and three identical cards. 'Unlock your potential'. Placeholder photos.

what stands out

The fixes

One accent colour you chose. Real content in your own words. Your own logo and screenshots. Plain layout with strong spacing. One idea per screen.

Say

  • This is exactly what our own center decided about its website and slides. We picked one accent colour, wrote real copy, and put the grain on top.
  • An AI tool gets you a working screen in a minute. Spend your time on the part it cannot do: specific words, a real brand, restraint.

Do

  • Show two screenshots, one generic generated page and one plain restrained one. Ask which they would trust.

Watch for

  • Do not turn this into a taste argument. The test is simple: could this page be for any product at all? If yes, it is generic.

The rulePick the simplest row that fits your demo and your team's skills. Every extra piece costs hours you do not have at hour 14.

Say

  • The first two rows you can finish in a hackathon. The third is what real products use, and it has more things that can go wrong.

  • Local is your laptop. Production is the live one, built from main.
  • Every time main changes, the host rebuilds and the URL updates.
  • A deploy that fails leaves the old version running. Read the build log.
Week 3, paid offIf main always works, the live site always works. That is the whole reason for the pull request rule.

Say

  • Deploying is just a computer somewhere else running your code and giving it an address. The host watches your repo and rebuilds when it changes.
  • That is why the pull request rule matters: if main always works, the live site always works.

Lab A: a page on GitHub Pages

  1. Open your hello-yourname repo from Week 1
  2. Settings, then Pages
  3. Source: Deploy from a branch. Branch: main, folder: / (root). Save
  4. Wait about a minute. Open the link it shows
  5. Open it on your phone. Edit index.html, push, refresh
your live URL
# the address looks like this
https://yourname.github.io/hello-yourname/
If it 404sThe repo must be public for this to work on a free account. Check there is an index.html at the top level.

Say

  • Five minutes of clicking and you have a real website. That is it. It is the same idea as every bigger host, minus the features.
  • The edit and refresh step is the point: change a word, push, and watch the live site change.

Do

  • Do it on your own account on the projector first. Then let them go.
  • Walk around checking that Pages is enabled on the right repo and the branch is set to main.

Watch for

  • A page that says 404 right after enabling usually just needs another minute. If it persists, index.html is missing from the top folder or the repo is private.

requirements.txt, one package per line
streamlit
requests
The usual fixIf the build fails, the log names the missing package. Add it to requirements.txt, push, and it rebuilds.

Say

  • Same idea, for Python. The host reads requirements.txt to know what to install. A missing line is the number one reason a deploy fails.
  • This is the live version of last week's weather app. Everyone gets a .streamlit.app address.

Do

  • Use a personal repo for this lab, not the org repo. Org repos may need the org owner to approve Streamlit's access, and that is a Week 6 question.
  • If the API is blocked for the host or school, switch the app to read sample.json so it still works.

Watch for

  • Build errors scare beginners. Read the log aloud with them: the last lines say what is missing.
  • Free plans have limits on how many apps and how long they sleep. Check the current Streamlit terms. Do not promise numbers from this slide.
02 — Deploying

Secrets, online

Streamlit: app settings, Secrets
# paste into the app's Secrets box, never commit it
MODEL_API_KEY = "paste-your-key-here"
reading it in the app
import streamlit as st

key = st.secrets["MODEL_API_KEY"]
  • Locally: .env. Online: the host's Secrets or Environment Variables page
  • The key never appears in your code or your repo
  • In Next.js, anything named NEXT_PUBLIC_... is sent to the browser. Never put a secret in one
Test itIf a secret is visible in the browser's developer tools, it is already public.

Say

  • Same rule as Week 2, now online. The host keeps the key in its own settings and gives it to your app when it runs.
  • The NEXT_PUBLIC point is the classic mistake: the name tells the framework to put the value in the page. A secret there is a public secret.

Watch for

  • Do not let anyone paste a real key into the demo. Make one up.
02 — Deploying

When the URL is wrong

What you seeLook here
Build failedThe build log. Last lines say which package or file is missing
Works locally, breaks onlineA file name with different capitals. Online systems are case-sensitive
Page loads, then errorsA missing secret or environment variable
Old version still showingDid the push reach main? Did the build finish?
Very slow the first timeFree apps sleep. Open it once before you demo

Say

  • These five show up again and again, so check them first. The case-sensitivity one catches people who build on Windows or Mac, where file names are usually not case-sensitive.
  • The last one is a demo killer: a sleeping app takes a long time to wake. Open it a few minutes before you present.
02 — Deploying

Preview links on every PR

  • Some hosts build a temporary live copy of each pull request, so a reviewer can click instead of running it.
  • That turns review from 'read the code' into 'use the thing'.
  • Check whether your host does this on a free plan and for a team repo, before you rely on it.
No preview links? A reviewer runs the branch on their laptop. Slower, but the rule is the same: someone uses it before it merges.

Say

  • Preview links are one of the best features of modern hosting, and one of the ones free plans restrict for teams. Find out what your host does.
  • Whatever you have, keep the rule: nothing merges until someone has used it.

Watch for

  • Do not demo a feature you have not tested with a team repo and two people committing. That is exactly where free plans surprise you.
